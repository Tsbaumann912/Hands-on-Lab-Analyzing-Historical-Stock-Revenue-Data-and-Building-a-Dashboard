# Module 08 — Discretionary Price Action & Microstructure (S23–S25)

**Edge hypothesis:** Short-horizon order imbalance and auction dynamics leave
footprints in price; skilled discretionary traders can read them — but the
edge is skill- and cost-sensitive, hard to scale, and easy to romanticize.

**Fails when:** Overconfidence, no stats, fighting institutional flow,
high fees.

---

## S23 — Price-action / supply-demand

### Mechanics

Trade market structure: swing highs/lows, break-of-structure, order blocks /
supply-demand zones, candlestick rejection at levels. Multi-timeframe:
higher TF bias, lower TF entry.

### Where it works

Liquid FX and futures charts; best as a **framework** combined with risk
rules, not as pattern memorization.

### Risk notes

- Subjective labels → unverifiable performance.
- Require a written ruleset and journal or you cannot improve.

## S24 — Order-flow / footprint concepts

### Mechanics

Use footprint charts, cumulative volume delta, depth-of-market, and time &
sales to infer absorption vs initiative buying/selling. Educational goal:
understand that **someone’s market order is someone else’s liquidity**.

### Where it works

Futures with reliable depth (CME); less transparent in fragmented spot FX.

### Risk notes

- Retail DOM without colocation is delayed relative to HFT.
- Easy to overfit narratives onto random tape.

### Videos & links

- CME education on electronic trading / order types: [cmegroup.com/education](https://www.cmegroup.com/education.html)

## S25 — Scalping & liquidity provision concepts

### Mechanics

Capture small edges from bid-ask, micro-mean-reversion, or inventory
management — closer to market-making than directional betting. Requires
ultra-low costs and discipline.

### Where it works

Professionally: exchange MMs and HFT. Retail scalping often loses to costs.

### Risk notes

- **This course does not teach latency arbitrage or abusive practices.**
- If round-trip costs > average scalp, expectancy is negative by definition.
